The problem

Small shop, big responsibility, no data team

A neighborhood grooming shop handles dozens of dogs a week, and every one of them needs a current rabies vaccination before it goes on the table. In a typical shop, that proof lives in a folder of paper certificates and a few emailed PDFs. The dog's last haircut, its allergies, and the fact that it hates having its back feet touched live in a groomer's memory or on an index card.

Nobody on staff is a database administrator, and nobody should have to be. But when the records are scattered, an expired vaccine slips through, a new groomer doesn't know about the allergy, and a regular's "the usual, please" means something different every visit.

What the shop gets

One screen at the front counter

Pick who's grooming, find the dog, and see at a glance whether it's cleared for today, and why not if it isn't.

Cleared for today?

Every dog shows a green check when its vaccines are current. If rabies has expired, the groom can't start, and the screen says why.

Paperwork in seconds

Snap the owner's certificate with the counter tablet. AI fills in the dates, and a person checks them before anything is saved.

Every dog's history

The usual style, last cut zone by zone, coat condition, allergies, and handling notes, all on the dog's card for whoever is grooming.

Calendar and booking

A month of grooms and upcoming vaccine expiries, with the ones that stop a groom in red. Book a dog with a groomer's free times.

Easy to fix, safe to change

Typos are corrected right on the card, and every change keeps its before and after, so nothing quietly disappears.

Manager sign-off

Sensitive changes, like removing an allergy, wait for a manager, who unlocks Admin with a fingerprint or PIN.

Try it in your browser

The foundation

The rules live in the database, not in anyone's memory

A small shop's system is first in the chain. There's no data team downstream to catch a bad row, so every rule a careful groomer would apply by judgment is written down as something the database itself will refuse. No screen, script, or tired employee can get around it:

  • A dog cannot start a groom while a required vaccine is expired, missing, or disputed.
  • A vaccination record cannot be created from a document that does not state an expiry date.
  • A shave-down cannot be recorded without a coat assessment that justifies it.
  • A haircut cannot be recorded on a bath-only visit.
  • A reminder cannot be sent to an owner who opted out.

How strictly each rule is enforced (block, warn, or off) is itself a setting the owner can change, and every change is recorded in the audit log.

The document that proves the point

A real vet invoice for Jaddi, the dog whose grooming routine started this project, names a rabies vaccination and the date it was given. It contains no expiry date anywhere on the page.

The system keeps the document, keeps what was read from it, and creates no vaccination record. It does not assume a one-year or three-year validity, because both are legal and the certificate is what the health department will actually see.

A system that correctly declines to invent a date is worth more to a business than one that always produces an answer.

Why a person still checks the AI

The AI that reads certificates was scored field by field against hand-labelled answer keys. Overconfident means a smudged character returned as a clean, specific value: a blurry Jan 2?, 2027 read back as Jan 29, 2027.

On clean pages it's perfect. On a phone photo, which is what a busy counter actually produces, it returns about fifteen confident dates the page doesn't support, and a better prompt didn't fix it. So the database doesn't trust a date just because one is present. A line becomes a vaccination record only after a person has checked it, and a date nobody can read becomes a request to the owner for a better copy, not a guess.
